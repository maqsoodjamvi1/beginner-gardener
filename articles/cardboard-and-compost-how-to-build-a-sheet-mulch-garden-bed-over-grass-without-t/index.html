Cardboard and Compost: How to Build a Sheet-Mulch Garden Bed Over Grass Without Tilling

Killing a lawn to start a new garden bed usually sounds like backbreaking work. I used to think I had to rent a sod cutter, hack away at tough turf, and spend my weekend hauling heavy clumps of grass to the curb. Then I discovered sheet mulching—often called lasagna gardening.

By layering plain cardboard and compost directly over your existing grass, you smother the turf without digging a single shovel blade into the ground. Over a few months, the grass and cardboard break down into rich organic matter, leaving you with a soft, fertile bed ready for planting. Here is how I build these beds in my own yard every spring.

Why Sheet Mulching Beats Tilling

When you fire up a rototiller to carve out a new garden bed, you aren’t just breaking soil. You are also waking up thousands of dormant weed seeds buried deep in the ground, bringing them to the surface where the sun can sprout them. Tilling also destroys the natural soil structure and disturbs the earthworms and beneficial microbes living just beneath the surface.

Sheet mulching takes the opposite approach. Instead of fighting the soil, you work with it. You block the sunlight completely so the grass dies and rots in place, feeding the very microbes you want in your garden. I find that beds made this way hold moisture much better during dry summer spells because the dense layers act like a giant sponge.

Gathering Your Materials

Before you lay down a single layer, you need to collect your supplies. Do not wait until halfway through the project to realize you are short on cardboard.

  • Plain cardboard: You need flat boxes with all the plastic tape, staples, and glossy colored ink removed. Appliance boxes or large shipping boxes work best because they overlap easily. Avoid corrugated plastic or wax-coated boxes, as they will not decompose.
  • Nitrogen-rich green layers: Fresh grass clippings, green yard waste, or aged manure add heat and speed up the breakdown of the cardboard.
  • Carbon-rich brown layers: Fall leaves, shredded straw, or untreated wood chips provide bulk and structure.
  • Bulk compost: You will need enough finished compost to create a solid three- to four-inch layer on top of the cardboard.

Step 1: Prep the Lawn and Edges

Start by mowing your grass as short as your lawnmower allows. You don’t need to bag the clippings; just let them settle into the turf.

Next, define the borders of your new bed. I like to use a flat-edged spade to trench a shallow, four-inch-deep groove along the perimeter of the bed. This creates a clean edge that stops grass runners from creeping into your new garden later, and it gives you a place to tuck the edges of your cardboard.

Step 2: Lay Down the Smother Layer

Water the grass thoroughly before you start. Dry soil makes for a slow decomposition process, so getting the ground soaked gives the microbes a head start.

Lay your cardboard directly over the grass, making sure the pieces overlap by at least six to eight inches. If you leave even small gaps, aggressive grasses like Bermuda grass or quackgrass will find the light and push right through. Cover the entire footprint of your bed, overlapping the edges slightly into the trench you dug earlier.

Soak this cardboard layer immediately with a garden hose until it is soggy. This weight keeps it from blowing away in the wind and helps mold it to the contours of the ground.

Step 3: Build the Compost and Mulch Layers

Immediately after wetting the cardboard, shovel your finished compost right on top. Spread it out into an even layer about three to four inches deep. This is the layer where you will plant your vegetables or flowers right away if you are in a rush, or you can let it sit and mellow.

On top of the compost, add a finishing layer of wood chips, shredded leaves, or straw to keep weeds from blowing in and taking root in the fresh compost. Keep this top mulch layer at least two to three inches deep. If you are building pathways around the bed, you can throw down extra wood chips there to keep your boots clean.

Managing the Waiting Period

If you build your sheet-mulch bed in the fall, you can walk away and let winter weather do the work. By spring, the grass, cardboard, and lower layers will have fused into a dark, crumbly earth that is easy to push your hands into.

If you build the bed in the middle of spring like I often do, you don’t even have to wait months to plant. You can slice an “X” through the soggy cardboard with your trowel, pull back the flaps, dig a small pocket into the native soil below, and drop in a tomato start or perennial flower. The surrounding cardboard will keep weeds down while the plant establishes its roots.

As you plan your next garden expansion, consider skipping the rental fees for heavy machinery and let cardboard and compost do the heavy lifting for you. Taking stock of your cardboard supply and mapping out your bed dimensions is a great way to start planning your next steps before the weekend arrives.
